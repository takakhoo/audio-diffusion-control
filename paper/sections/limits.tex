\section{Limitations and Outlook}

No listening test was run. Every claim about quality rests on two learned predictors and every claim about meaning on signal descriptors and tags, and these are proxies: energy decay time is confounded by sustained instruments, and SongEval was trained on songs with vocals. Most clips are ten seconds long. The usable-span tolerance of 0.5 CE is a choice. Coverage depends on our prompt list and on FMA as the sample of real music, and both embedding models were trained on real recordings, so generated audio may be slightly out of their distribution. Axis labels come from an 80-word vocabulary, and a different vocabulary would name the same direction differently. The internal-axis result is for token-averaged cross-attention outputs of an eight-step distilled model; other sites in the network may hold what this one does not.

What we take from the study is a way of working rather than a set of sliders. Decide what a control should change before training it, measure that thing from the waveform, let a quality model anchored to real recordings say how far the control may be pushed, and let real music, rather than a prompt writer, suggest which axes are worth a control at all.

\section{Ethics Statement}

The real recordings used to find axes come from the Free Music Archive, which is released under Creative Commons licenses, and only embeddings and direction vectors derived from them are redistributed. The generators are open-weight models used under their licenses. Sliders of this kind lower the effort needed to produce many variations of a generated piece; they do not change what the underlying models can produce, and they inherit those models' biases, including the narrowness along musical axes that this paper measures. We release code, trained sliders, and the evaluation clips so that the measurements can be checked and extended.
