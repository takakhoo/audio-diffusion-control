\section{Introduction}

Anyone who has worked with a text-to-music model knows the moment. The clip is good, almost right, and you want it a little happier, or a little fuller, or with richer harmony. The only control you have is the prompt, and rewriting it hands you a different piece. What a musician wants here is closer to a knob than a sentence: keep the prompt, keep the seed, and move the result along one axis by an amount you choose.

Image diffusion already has such knobs. Concept Sliders \citep{gandikota2024sliders} train a low-rank adapter whose scale is set at inference time, and SliderSpace \citep{gandikota2025sliderspace} goes further and discovers the axes themselves. Both ideas have crossed into audio, mostly as baselines \citep{ezra2025freesliders,staniszewski2026tada}. In that work a slider counts as working if the output moves toward the attribute text in a joint text--audio embedding and stays close to the original.

For music this leaves a lot unmeasured. An embedding can move while the thing a listener would name does not; we will show two sliders for which exactly this happens. A slider can also reach its target by wrecking the clip, and similarity to the original says nothing about whether what remains is still music. Nothing in the score says what else moved. And the axes themselves are chosen by whoever writes the prompt pair, with no evidence that these are the directions along which music varies, or that the generator can reach their ends at all.

This paper is about measuring those things, and about what happens when you let real music pick the axes instead. We work with two open generators of different design, a 50-step latent diffusion model and an 8-step rectified-flow model, and with a third as a check. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A measurement protocol for music sliders.} Every control is scored on a waveform descriptor fixed in advance, in two unrelated embedding models, by two unrelated quality predictors anchored to real recordings, on similarity to the unsteered piece, and on what else it moved. Effects are reported only over the \emph{usable span}, the positions at which quality has not yet dropped.
\item \textbf{A comparison of four ways to move the same attribute.} A trained slider reproduces prompt-pair guidance with one forward pass per step instead of three; activation steering is a real competitor; interpolating the text conditioning fails.
\item \textbf{Axes taken from real music, and how much of them a generator covers.} Independent components of embeddings of \covReal{} recordings include the arousal and valence of the circumplex model \citep{russell1980circumplex}. ACE-Step's output over 648 prompts spans \covLo{}--\covHi{}\% of the real spread along them, and Stable Audio Open looks the same.
\item \textbf{Sliders along those axes, trained two ways.} From a prompt pair made of the axis's own tags, and with no text from two sets of the generator's clips. Both follow the axis. The first moves further; the second keeps more of the piece, moves less of everything else, and a graded form of it reaches $\pm2$.
\end{enumerate}
We also report what did not work, because the negative results shape the method: training directly between sets of real recordings, and principal axes of the generator's own activations, which turned out to describe production rather than music.
